## 0. Summary

Program (a159-a169): for a map P of F_q^k let A_t(x) = P(x) + tx and

    Lambda(P) = sum_{t in F_q} H(X | A_t(X))      (bits, X uniform on F_q^k).

By Theorem 2 (README), the Kakeya growth constant of F_q is beta_q = q 2^(-lambda*/q), where
lambda* = sup_k max_P Lambda(P)/k. The conjecture C_q(k), Lambda <= k(q-1) for all k, says
lambda* = q - 1, the value of the frame products (x_1^2, ..., x_k^2). Bukh-Chao (2021) and
Dvir-Kopparty-Saraf-Sudan give lambda* <= q log2(2 - 1/q).

The earlier tools in this program were information-theoretic (chain rule, Jensen, peeling,
averaging). This note adds an algebraic-geometry tool: Bezout's theorem with intersection
multiplicities, applied to one fibre of A_t at a time. We call a polynomial map P
*nondegenerate* if its top-degree forms have no common zero in P^(k-1) over the algebraic
closure of F_q. Write c_t(y) = dim ker(DP(y) + tI) for the corank of the Jacobian of A_t at y,
and

    g(J) = sum_{t in F_q} dim ker(J + tI) = sum_{lambda in F_q} dim ker(J - lambda I)  (<= k),

the number of F_q-eigenvalues of J counted by geometric multiplicity. g(J) = k exactly when J
is diagonalizable over F_q.

> **Lemma 125 (fibre budget).** Let P be nondegenerate with deg P_i = d_i >= 2. For every t
> and every fibre F of A_t,
>
>     sum_{y in F} 2^(c_t(y)) <= d_1 d_2 ... d_k.

> **Theorem 50 (Bezout-Jacobian bound).** For every nondegenerate polynomial map P with all
> deg P_i = d_i >= 2,
>
>     Lambda(P) <= q sum_i log2 d_i - E_X g(DP(X)).
>
> For quadratic maps this reads Lambda(P) <= k(q - 1) + delta(P), where
> delta(P) = k - E_X g(DP(X)) >= 0 is the expected number of F_q-eigenvalues that the
> Jacobian is *missing*.

> **Corollary 50.1.** C_q(k) holds for every nondegenerate quadratic map whose Jacobian
> DP(x) is diagonalizable over F_q at every point x. A nondegenerate quadratic map that
> breaks C_q(k) must have E g(DP(X)) < k - (Lambda - k(q-1)): its Jacobian is not split
> over F_q on a positive proportion of points.

> **Proposition 57 (sharpness).** For every frame product P_i(x) = a_i x_i^2 + l_i x_i + c_i
> (a_i != 0, q odd), Lemma 125 is an equality in *every* fibre and Theorem 50 is an equality:
> Lambda = k(q - 1) = qk - E g.

So the conjecture's extremal maps are exactly tight for a bound that comes from a completely
different source than the earlier tools, and the bound attributes the whole "missing k bits"
of the conjecture to one mechanism: points where the Jacobian of A_t is singular, which take
more than their share of the Bezout budget 2^k.

The bound is weak for maps whose Jacobians lack F_q-eigenvalues (Propositions 58-59): Bezout
counts all points over the algebraic closure, and irrational ones take budget without
appearing in a fibre. **Nothing here proves a new Kakeya bound.** What it adds is a sharp
"split case" to set against Theorem 48 (a169), which is strong exactly when fibres are
small and isotropic.

## 1. Proofs

*Proof of Lemma 125.* Fix t and x, and let F be the fibre of A_t through x. Consider the
system f(y) = A_t(y) - A_t(x) = 0 of k polynomial equations in k unknowns. Since d_i >= 2,
the top-degree part of f_i is the top-degree form of P_i (the term t y_i has degree 1). By
nondegeneracy, the homogenized system has no zero on the hyperplane at infinity. Its zero
set in P^k is therefore a projective variety inside affine space, hence finite, and by
Bezout's theorem the intersection multiplicities of all its points over the algebraic
closure add up to exactly d_1 ... d_k. The points of F are among them.

At y in F, the Jacobian of f is DP(y) + tI, of corank c = c_t(y). Replace f by Gf for an
invertible constant matrix G (same ideal, same multiplicities) so that the last c
components have zero linear part at y. The first k - c components then cut out a smooth
germ W of dimension c at y, and the last c components restrict to W as functions of order
at least 2. So the local intersection multiplicity at y is at least 2^c (Fulton,
*Intersection Theory*, Cor. 12.4). Summing over y in F gives the claim. ∎

*Proof of Theorem 50.* Let D = d_1 ... d_k. For a fibre F, Lemma 125 gives
|F| avg_{y in F} 2^(c(y)) <= D. Since 2^c is convex, avg 2^c >= 2^(avg c), so
log2 |F| <= log2 D - avg_{y in F} c(y). Then

    H(X | A_t(X)) = sum_F (|F|/q^k) log2 |F| <= log2 D - E_X c_t(X),

because X is uniform, so averaging over fibres weighted by size is averaging over points.
Summing over t gives sum_t c_t(x) = g(DP(x)), and the bound follows. The stronger form
before Jensen, Lambda <= sum_t sum_F (|F|/q^k)(log2 D - log2 avg_F 2^c), is also checked
by the tests. For quadratic maps D = 2^k and q k - E g = k(q - 1) + (k - E g). ∎

*Proof of Corollary 50.1.* If DP(x) is diagonalizable over F_q, its F_q-eigenspaces span
F_q^k, so g(DP(x)) = k. ∎

*Remark (k = 1).* A 1 x 1 matrix always has its eigenvalue in F_q. So every quadratic map
of F_q has g = 1 at every point, and Theorem 50 gives Lambda <= q - 1. This is C_q(1) for
quadratics again, now as a special case of Bezout.

*Proof of Proposition 57.* DP(x) = diag(2 a_i x_i + l_i) is diagonal, so g = k at every
point. The fibre of A_t through x is the product of the one-dimensional fibres
{x_i, -(l_i + t)/a_i - x_i}. A coordinate is a singleton exactly when 2 a_i x_i + l_i + t = 0,
and then it contributes corank 1. So every point of a fibre has the same c = number of
singleton coordinates, the fibre has 2^(k - c) points, and sum_F 2^c = 2^k. Jensen is an
equality because c is constant on fibres. Lambda = k(q - 1) is the product formula. ∎

## 2. Examples: where the bound is weak, and why

**Proposition 58 (the swap map).** P(x) = (x_2^2, x_1^2) on F_q^2 (q odd) is nondegenerate,
and sum_x g(DP(x)) = q^2 + 1, so Theorem 50 gives Lambda <= 2q - 1 - 1/q^2.

*Proof.* DP(x) = [[0, 2x_2], [2x_1, 0]] has characteristic polynomial lambda^2 - 4 x_1 x_2.
If x_1 x_2 is a nonzero square, there are two distinct F_q-eigenvalues and g = 2
((q-1)^2/2 points). If it is a non-square, g = 0 ((q-1)^2/2 points). If exactly one
coordinate is 0, DP(x) is a nonzero nilpotent matrix and g = 1 (2(q-1) points). At x = 0,
g = 2. The total is (q-1)^2 + 2(q-1) + 2 = q^2 + 1. ∎

So Theorem 50 misses C_q(2) for this map by about one bit (C_q(2) holds for all quadratic
maps of the plane by Theorem 38). The missing bit is the half of the points where the
Jacobian has no F_q-eigenvalue.

**Proposition 59 (squaring in F_(q^k)).** For P(z) = z^2 on F_(q^k), with k = 2 or 3 and q
odd, sum_x g(DP(x)) = kq, so Theorem 50 gives Lambda <= qk - k q^(1-k), while the true value
is Lambda = q - q^(1-k) (a168, Proposition 54).

*Proof.* DP(z) is multiplication by 2z. For z in F_q it is a scalar matrix, so g = k. For z
not in F_q, 2z generates F_(q^k) (k is prime), so the characteristic polynomial is
irreducible of degree k and g = 0. ∎

Here every fibre {z, -t-z} has 2 rational points, and the other 2^k - 2 Bezout points are
conjugate under Frobenius. Theorem 50 cannot see them. Theorem 48 (a169) handles this map
instead.

## 3. What this changes in the inverse problem

Taken together, Theorems 48 and 50 say that a quadratic counterexample to C_q(k) has to be
two things at once. It must have large fibres of three or more points (Corollary 48.2), which
are spread over many directions (Theorem 48). And, if it is nondegenerate, its Jacobian must
be non-split on a positive proportion of points (Corollary 50.1). Each fibre still has at most
2^k points (Lemma 125).

In the language of a169, Theorem 49: a nondegenerate quadratic map has Lambda <= kL whenever
E g(DP(X)) >= k(q - L). At q = 3 and the record threshold L = 2.21, that needs
E g >= 0.79 k.

*Heuristic remark (not proved).* There seem to be three ways for a map to lose bits relative
to the trivial qk log2 d. (i) **Collisions**: singular Jacobians use up extra Bezout budget,
which Theorem 50 measures exactly. (ii) **Degeneracy at infinity**: fibre points escape to
infinity or lie on curves, as for G_n of a167 and the dual-number square
(x_1^2, 2 x_1 x_2). (iii) **Galois**: fibre points over the algebraic closure that are not
rational, as in Proposition 59. For fixed k and large q, a Chebotarev-type count suggests
that (iii) costs on the order of q bits unless the whole fibre cover splits over F_q. In the
split case, the branch points are F_q-rational eigenvalues, and (i) is the only loss left.
Making this precise would prove C_q(k) for nondegenerate quadratic maps when q is large
compared with k. That is the next target for this tool. It does not reach the
fixed-q, growing-k regime that sets beta_q.

## 4. Status

- **Proved here:** Lemma 125, Theorem 50 (every q, every k, all degrees >= 2), Corollary 50.1,
  Propositions 57-59.
- **Not proved:** C_q(k) for quadratic maps with k >= 4, (*) or weak (*), and any new
  Kakeya bound.
- **Prior art:** we found no use of per-fibre Bezout multiplicities, or of the Jacobian's
  F_q-spectrum, in the finite-field Kakeya literature (Dvir 2008; Saraf-Sudan;
  Dvir-Kopparty-Saraf-Sudan; Bukh-Chao 2021; Zahl's 2025 survey arXiv:2512.09397). The method
  of multiplicities there is global: one polynomial vanishing to high order on the whole set.
